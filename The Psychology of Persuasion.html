Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion

How to Persuade Others and Protect Yourself from Manipulation | By Robert B. Cialdini

Part I: Reciprocation, Commitment & Consistency
1. Reciprocation & The Rejection-Then-Retreat Method
Core Principle People are psychologically wired to repay favors, gifts, or concessions. This obligation often compels individuals to agree to a larger return request than the initial gesture.
Technique / Practice Door-in-the-Face (Rejection-Then-Retreat) — make an extreme initial request that will likely be rejected, then follow up with your actual (smaller) request as a perceived concession.
Actionable Rule Provide genuine value or a concession first to build goodwill, but actively recognize unasked-for favors designed solely to trigger compliance.
2. Commitment & Consistency
Core Principle Once people take a stand or commit to an action (especially publicly or in writing), they experience strong cognitive pressure to align future behaviors with that commitment.
Technique / Practice Foot-in-the-Door Technique — secure a small, initial agreement to establish an identity or stance, making the target exponentially more likely to comply with a larger related request later.
Actionable Rule Get clients or team members to make active, public, and voluntary commitments (e.g., written goals or micro-conversions) to secure long-term follow-through.
Part II: Social Proof, Liking & Unity
3. Social Proof
Core Principle When uncertain, individuals look to the actions and behaviors of similar others to guide their own decisions, assuming the crowd possesses superior knowledge.
Technique / Practice Peer Validation Framing — leverage user testimonials, subscriber counts, case studies, and relatable peer examples to eliminate perceived risk and decision paralysis.
Actionable Rule Demonstrate that people similar to your target audience are already adopting your product, idea, or behavior.
4. Liking & Unity
Core Principle People prefer to say yes to individuals they know, like, or identify with as part of an shared in-group ("We" identity). Liking is driven by physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, and cooperation.
Technique / Practice In-Group Identity Framing — highlight shared background, common struggles, genuine compliments, and collaborative goals to create immediate rapport and trust.
Actionable Rule Uncover and emphasize authentic similarities and shared group identities with your audience before proposing solutions or requests.
Part III: Authority, Scarcity & Defense
5. Authority
Core Principle Human beings are conditioned to defer to perceived experts, credentials, titles, and symbols of authority without critical evaluation.
Technique / Practice Credibility Signalling — present credentials, certifications, third-party endorsements, or objective domain expertise early in an interaction before delivering the core message.
Actionable Rule Establish domain authority transparently, and continuously ask yourself: "Is this authority figure truly an expert on this specific topic?"
6. Scarcity
Core Principle Opportunities seem significantly more valuable when their availability is limited. Loss aversion dictates that the fear of losing something is a stronger motivator than the prospect of gaining something of equal value.
Technique / Practice Loss Framing & Exclusive Access — emphasize what will be lost by inaction, utilizing genuine time-limited deadlines or restricted supply limits.
Actionable Rule Frame proposals in terms of potential losses rather than just gains, ensuring all scarcity claims remain authentic to maintain long-term trust.

Master Implementation Checklist

  • Trigger Reciprocity First: Provide real value, helpful content, or genuine assistance before making an ask.
  • Secure Public Commitments: Encouraging micro-steps or written pledges builds long-term alignment and consistency.
  • Showcase Authentic Social Proof: Use clear, peer-driven metrics and real user testimonials to lower decision friction.
  • Establish True Authority & Scarcity: Highlight valid credentials and communicate legitimate constraints or exclusive opportunities.
